*Small steps, big milestones. Helping your child build independence, one success at a time.*

Toilet training can be a big milestone for any family. For parents of autistic children, the process may come with some additional challenges.

Maybe your child doesn’t seem to notice when they’re wet. Maybe they refuse to sit on the toilet. Or perhaps you’ve tried several approaches, and nothing seems to work.

Here’s the good news: Toilet training is a skill that can be taught, practiced, and developed over time.

At Sunbird ABA, we believe that every child learns differently. With patience, consistency, and the right support, families can help their children build toileting skills at a pace that works for them.

## 1. Start with your child’s readiness for potty training

Before beginning toilet training, observe your child’s current skills and comfort level.

Ask yourself:

- Does my child notice when their diaper or clothing is wet?
- Can they sit comfortably on the toilet, even briefly?
- Do they show signs before needing to use the bathroom?
- Can they communicate discomfort or needs through words, gestures, pictures, or a communication device?

Your child doesn’t need to master all these skills before getting started. These observations simply help you understand where to begin.

## 2. Make bathroom trips part of the daily routine

Many autistic children benefit from predictability.

Instead of waiting for your child to tell you they need the bathroom, begin offering opportunities at natural times throughout the day.

For example:

- After waking up
- After meals
- Before leaving the house
- Before bedtime

Keep bathroom visits brief and comfortable. If your child becomes distressed, adjust the approach rather than forcing them to sit.

Over time, predictable bathroom routines can help your child become familiar with the process.

## 3. Use visual supports for the toilet routine

Sometimes pictures communicate more clearly than words.

Try creating a simple visual sequence showing:

1. Go to the bathroom.
2. Pull down pants.
3. Sit on the toilet.
4. Wipe and get dressed.
5. Flush when comfortable.
6. Wash hands.

You can use pictures, photographs, or simple drawings.

For children who use limited spoken language, visuals can make the routine easier to understand.

## 4. Celebrate small successes with positive reinforcement

Positive reinforcement is a valuable teaching strategy.

Don’t wait until your child successfully uses the toilet to recognize progress.

Celebrate steps such as:

- Walking into the bathroom willingly
- Sitting comfortably on the toilet
- Following part of the bathroom routine
- Communicating that they need to go
- Successfully using the toilet

A favorite song, sticker, high-five, or another activity your child enjoys can make practicing more motivating.

Choose encouragement that your child actually likes, and never make basic care or comfort dependent on toileting success.

## 5. Consider sensory challenges in the bathroom

For some autistic children, the bathroom itself can feel overwhelming.

Think about the environment:

- Is the flushing sound frightening?
- Does the toilet seat feel uncomfortable?
- Are the bathroom lights too bright?
- Do their feet need additional support?

Small adjustments can make a meaningful difference.

A child-sized toilet seat, footstool, quieter environment, or waiting to flush until your child leaves may help make bathroom visits more comfortable.

## 6. Handle potty training accidents calmly

Accidents are part of learning.

Avoid punishment, embarrassment, or showing disappointment.

Instead, help your child get cleaned up comfortably and return to their routine.

The goal isn’t perfection. It’s helping your child feel safe, confident, and increasingly independent.

If toileting is painful, your child frequently holds their stool, or constipation is an ongoing concern, speak with their pediatrician before continuing a training plan.

## 7. Keep everyone on the same page

Toilet training often works best when parents, caregivers, teachers, and therapists use a consistent approach.

Consider discussing:

- When bathroom opportunities will be offered
- Which words or pictures everyone will use
- How progress will be encouraged
- How to recognize signs of discomfort
- What to do when accidents happen

A coordinated approach helps children practice the same skills across settings.

## How can ABA therapy help with toilet training?

Applied behavior analysis (ABA) can support toilet training by breaking a complex routine into smaller, manageable skills.

A Board Certified Behavior Analyst (BCBA) can help identify barriers, develop individualized teaching strategies, support communication, and monitor progress.
